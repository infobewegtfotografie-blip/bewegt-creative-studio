Take a postcard from this period. Turn it over.

On the front, a photograph — a market, a portrait, a street in Freetown or Lomé. On the back, a printed line, a serial number, a publisher's name. Nothing indicates who took the photograph. Often, no one knows anymore.

It is the most ordinary object in the history of photography in Africa, and one of the most consequential. At the end of the [previous episode](/blog/freetown-fernando-po-accra-west-african-photographers.html), we left the West African photographers in command of their studios but dispossessed of the fate of their images. The postcard is the object where this question plays out at industrial scale — and, in the same movement, a trade in which some of them thrived.

Both things are true at once. That is the whole interest of the period.

## Why 1900?

*Technical and commercial conditions, 1893-1914*

Two processes make the illustrated postcard commercially viable between 1893 and 1920: lithography and, above all, collotype (phototype), which reproduces a photograph's tonal range with a fineness close to the original print.[^1] A photographic image can now be reproduced in tens of thousands of copies for almost nothing.

The centre of gravity of this industry is German: German printers dominate the world market for quality postcards, working as subcontractors for foreign publishers, until the war cuts these circuits in 1914.[^1] Collectors call 1900-1914 the golden age of the postcard.

The material detail has a considerable consequence. A negative made in Lomé could be sent to a European printer to be turned, by collotype or lithography, into hundreds or thousands of postcards. The printed copies then came back to Africa, were sold in trading posts, shops and studios, and set off again with travellers, administrators, merchants or collectors. The production chain of the African image now stretched across several territories. Between the shot and the recipient's gaze stood the publisher, the printer, the caption, the serial number and the market.

*Negatives head to European printers; the printed cards come back, sell locally, then set off for good with travellers and collectors. A schematic map, with no geographic precision. Original infographic, BEWEGT CREATIVE STUDIO.*

The reference work on this production is that of historian **Christraud M. Geary**, curator at the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, who devoted two books to it twenty years apart: *Delivering Views* (1998), co-edited with Virginia-Lee Webb, then *Postcards from Africa* (2018), drawn from the Leonard A. Lauder collection held at the MFA.[^2]

Geary traces the biographies of the European and African practitioners who fed this trade across the French, German and British empires. Among them, **François-Edmond Fortier** (1862-1928) is the best-documented case in French West Africa. The Metropolitan Museum describes him as one of the most prolific European photographers in the region: active between 1900 and 1912, he produced at least three thousand negatives, many of them printed as postcards he kept reissuing into the early 1920s.[^3] The Met also specifies the orientation of his work — an ethnographic approach, aimed almost exclusively at foreign communities settled in Africa and at his compatriots back in Europe.[^3]

This last point deserves emphasis. Fortier does not photograph for the Africans he photographs. His clientele is elsewhere, and that determines everything: the choice of subjects, the staging, and above all how they are named.

One detail usefully complicates the portrait: Fortier, born in the Vosges in 1862, died in Dakar in 1928.[^3] The man who sold French West Africa to Europeans spent his life there, and is buried there.

## What About African Photographers?

*Lisk-Carew, Acolatse, 1900-1930*

They are in this trade. Not at its margin: at its centre.

Take the **Lisk-Carew brothers** again, whose settling in Freetown in the early 1900s we followed earlier. Their studio did not stop at portraits. It published its own postcards — a production estimated at more than three thousand, with historian Julie Crooks herself pointing out the difficulty of establishing an exact number — and also sold stationery and novelty items.[^4] Their cards are held today in the Library of Congress's Africana Historic Postcard Collection; the Metropolitan Museum holds their group portrait *Bundoo Girls, Sierra Leone*.[^4]

A West African studio, run by two Creole brothers, self-publishing several thousand postcards for an international market: the line "Europeans took control of the African image" does not hold as it stands.

The second case picks up directly where episode 3 left off. **Alex Agbaglo Acolatse** (1880-1975) trained within the network of Lutterodt photographers, the Accra dynasty whose itinerant studios along the coast we followed earlier, then set up his own practice in Togo around 1900. Active for several decades — the Metropolitan Museum holds negatives dated 1909, the 1910s and the 1920s — he photographed the elites and the social and political life of the country, made numerous self-portraits, and also published postcards, notably in the 1920s.[^5] His work is held today at the Met and at the Museum Rietberg in Zurich.[^5]

A Togolese photographer, trained by a Ghanaian family, documenting his country's society for half a century and selling part of his output on the postcard market. The West African network described in the previous episode did not collapse in the face of the European industry. It plugged into it.

## What the Caption Does to the Image

*The shift of meaning, 1900-1920*

This is where another form of control over the image plays out: no longer only in the shot, but in the text that accompanies it.

A studio photograph shows a person who chose their clothing, their pose, and the moment of being photographed. Printed as a postcard, numbered within a series and captioned with an ethnonym, the same image stops being a portrait. It becomes a specimen.

This is not a theoretical reconstruction. Here are real captions, as they appear on cards held at the Metropolitan Museum of Art.

### The Caption, Word for Word

> **"Afrique Occidentale — Jeunes Filles Ouolof" [West Africa — Young Wolof Girls]**
> Postcard, 1900-1912, attributed to François-Edmond Fortier.[^6]

> **"Afrique Occidentale — Danseurs 'Miniankas' — Fétiches des Cultures" [West Africa — 'Minianka' Dancers — Cult Fetishes]**
> Postcard, 1905-1906, François-Edmond Fortier.[^7]

Look at what these few words accomplish.

**The place disappears.** "Afrique Occidentale" designates French West Africa — more than four million square kilometres, eight territories. No city, no village, no address. The photograph was indeed taken somewhere; the caption dissolves it.

**The name disappears.** No one is named. In its place, an ethnonym — "Wolof," "Minianka" — that replaces identity with belonging. These women are not people who agreed to be photographed: they are specimens of a category.

**The plural takes over.** "Young Girls," "Dancers." Never one young girl, never one dancer. The grammar itself forbids the individual.

**The vocabulary judges.** "Fétiches" is not a neutral descriptive term: it is a word from colonial literature, one that classifies a religious practice as superstition.

The operation is purely editorial. No retouching, no cropping: a line of text and a serial number are enough. This is why the colonial postcard is such a troubling object to study — the photograph it contains can be technically excellent, and sometimes even the fruit of a genuine understanding between the photographer and their subject, while the apparatus surrounding it turns it inside out entirely.

It is the question raised about the portrait of King Taki II in the previous episode — *who chose the pose, who looks at the lens* — shifted one notch further. Here, the one who decides is neither the photographer nor the subject.

## What Changed

*Stock-taking, 1900-1920*

Between 1865 and 1905, the question was: who holds the camera? The answer had increasingly become: an African.

Between 1900 and 1920, the question shifts: who decides what the image means, and how far it travels? African studios produce, publish and sell — but within an economy whose presses, catalogues and descriptive vocabulary lie elsewhere.

Beware the easy conclusion. Lisk-Carew and Acolatse are not victims of the postcard trade: they are entrepreneurs who used it, made a living from it, and circulated their images at a scale no nineteenth-century studio could have reached. But their name tends to fade along the way — even in today's catalogues, where the collector's name sometimes precedes the photographer's.

In the following decades, the centre of gravity will gradually shift. A new generation of studios will work first for urban African clienteles, who will come to be photographed for themselves, their families and their communities. In Bamako, Dakar or Kinshasa, the studio will become one of the places where modern African societies make their own image.

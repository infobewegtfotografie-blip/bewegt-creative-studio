In the first episode of this series, the daguerreotype reached Africa by the most expected route: that of the orientalist travellers sent to Egypt as early as November 1839, then the colonial shipping lanes that carried it, seven years later, as far as the Cape. West Africa, by contrast, received the process by two almost contradictory paths — one carried by French commercial administration, the other by a man who chose exile to escape the second-class citizenship his own country reserved for him.

## A customs officer on assignment, a "missed shot" in Saint-Louis

Jules Alphonse Eugène Itier (1802-1877) was neither an artist nor a scholar by trade: born into a family of customs officers, he entered the customs administration in 1819 in Marseille, under the protection of his uncle Dubois-Aymé, a former École Polytechnique student and member of Bonaparte's Egyptian expedition.[^1] In 1842, Itier set off on a series of missions meant to promote French trade and colonial expansion — to Senegal, then Guiana, then the West Indies.[^1][^2]

<figure>
<img src="/img/blog/jules-itier-autoportrait-mandarin-1847.jpg" alt="Jules Itier dressed as a mandarin, self-portrait daguerreotype, Marseille, 1847">
<figcaption>Jules Itier in 1847, dressed as a mandarin, self-portrait daguerreotype made in Marseille on his return from his mission to China. Wikimedia Commons — public domain.[^3]</figcaption>
</figure>

It was in Saint-Louis, Senegal, that Itier noted in his travel logbook a line that has since become a landmark in the history of West African photography: "I received my daguerreotype."[^1] Art historian Giulia Paoletti (University of Virginia), who has devoted more than a decade to this history — including three years living in Senegal and more than 100 interviews — places in this same period what is today considered the earliest known daguerreotype of West Africa: a view of the port of Saint-Louis showing a few colonial buildings, a square, and two blurred figures straying into the frame — a shot Itier himself described as a "missed shot."[^4]

Sources do not fully agree on the exact date of this view — 1842 according to coverage of Paoletti's book, 1843 according to Itier's own logbook — a discrepancy that illustrates how difficult it is to pin down a fine-grained chronology for this period.[^1][^4] What is better established is the rest of Itier's journey: by the end of 1843, he had been appointed to head a commercial mission accompanying Théodore de Lagrené's embassy to China, where he would produce one of the very first photographic bodies of work of the country. More than thirty-six plates and over a thousand pages of his travel notebooks are today held at the Musée français de la photographie, in Bièvres.[^5]

## Portrait and Place: a history still being written

The fate of this inaugural image sums up, in Paoletti's view, a larger problem: nineteenth-century Senegalese photography went largely ignored by art history for more than a century. "Initially, it wasn't considered an art anywhere — even in the West, it took many decades before we had the first exhibitions at MoMA," she explains. "And since it was not considered an art, it was not collected, studied, and valued as a craft."[^6] Her book *Portrait and Place: Photography in Senegal, 1840-1960* (Princeton University Press, 2024) is to date the first in-depth study of this history — from Itier's plate to Senegalese photographers of the 1960s, by way of rediscovered figures such as Mama Casset and Oumar Ka.[^6]

This asymmetry — a French daguerreotype as the documented starting point, a *Senegalese* history of photography that is written only nearly two centuries later — already announces the question that will run through this whole series: from what point can we speak of a truly *African* photography, rather than simply a photograph taken *in* Africa?

## Monrovia, 1853: Augustus Washington chooses exile

Thousands of kilometres away, on the coast of Liberia, another story begins — and it has nothing colonial about it. Augustus Washington (born around 1820-1821), the son of an African American father and a mother probably of Asian descent, was in his freshman year at Dartmouth College in the winter of 1843 when he turned to daguerreotype photography to fund his studies.[^7] He soon left Dartmouth and, within five years, had built a thriving studio in Hartford, Connecticut. By 1850, he was a prosperous photographer and a husband.

<figure>
<img src="/img/blog/carte-liberia-mitchell-1839.jpg" alt="Map of the colony of Liberia by S. Augustus Mitchell, 1839">
<figcaption>S. Augustus Mitchell, <em>Map of Liberia colony</em>, from <em>Mitchell's School Atlas</em>, 1839. Wikimedia Commons — public domain. Washington opened his studio in Monrovia, the colony's capital shown here on the coast, in 1853.[^8]</figcaption>
</figure>

Washington was acutely aware of this. In an 1851 letter to the *New York Tribune*, he wrote: "Strange as it may appear, whatever may be a colored man's natural capacity and literary attainments, I believe that, as soon as he leaves the academic halls to mingle in the only society he can find in the United States, unless he be a minister or lecturer, he must and will retrograde."[^7] In the climate of fear that followed the passage of the Fugitive Slave Act, Washington chose emigration over second-class citizenship. In November 1853, with his wife and two young children, he sailed for Monrovia, the capital of Liberia, under the auspices of the American Colonization Society.[^7]

With money saved in Hartford, Washington set up a daguerreotype studio in Monrovia as soon as he arrived. He quickly became the portraitist of the young republic's political elite — legislators and executive officials — while, between bouts of malaria, turning to trade, real estate, then sugarcane farming, eventually growing a first ten-acre plot into a thousand-acre estate upriver from Monrovia.[^7][^9]

<figcaption>Augustus Washington, <em>Chancy Brown</em>, Sergeant at Arms of the Liberian Senate, sixth-plate daguerreotype, between 1856 and 1858. Library of Congress, Prints &amp; Photographs Division — no known restrictions on publication.[^10]</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure>
<img src="/img/blog/femme-urias-mcgill-augustus-washington-liberia-1855.jpg" alt="Daguerreotype of a woman probably identified as Sarah McGill Russwurm, by Augustus Washington">

These two plates, held at the Library of Congress, give a sense of Washington's eye. In both, what is striking is how directly the sitters meet the camera, entirely at ease — an unselfconsciousness that historians of African American photography partly attribute to the fact that Washington was photographing his own peers, not subjects he might, like so many European daguerreotypists of the same decade, have treated as mere ethnographic curiosities. The Library of Congress holds, from the same studio, several other figures of the republic — including Stephen Allen Benson, Liberia's second president.[^10]

<figure>
<img src="/img/blog/residence-president-roberts-liberia-1850.jpg" alt="Engraving of the residence of Joseph Roberts, President of Liberia, c. 1850">
<figcaption>Residence of Joseph Roberts, first President of the Republic of Liberia, engraving by T. Williams, c. 1850. Wikimedia Commons — public domain. Washington photographed this same political elite through the 1850s.[^12]</figcaption>
</figure>

Many of his portraits, commissioned by the American Colonization Society itself, were meant to be shown in the United States to make the case for the recolonisation project.[^9] The last known trace of Washington active behind the camera dates from the late 1850s. He then began a political career: he sat twice in Liberia's House of Representatives — where he was elected speaker — and served one term in the Senate.[^7] He died in Monrovia in 1875; the *African Repository* marked his passing by noting that "nothing could induce him to return to this country [America], having acquired a handsome property and freedom and a home in his ancestral land."[^7]

## Two routes, not yet an African photography

Itier and Washington have, on the surface, nothing in common: one documents a commercial stopover in passing, on behalf of French expansion; the other deliberately builds his life and fortune in West Africa, becomes a citizen there, then a political leader. But neither one is, in the sense used by historians specialising in African photography, an *African* photographer: Itier remains an official passing through, and Washington, though a Liberian by choice and by law, remains an African American trained in Hartford, practising an imported craft.

That is precisely the distinction historian Jürg Schneider draws when he places the emergence of a photography *produced by Africans, for local clienteles* only in the second half of the nineteenth century — with figures such as Francis W. Joaque, active in Sierra Leone, Fernando Po, and then Gabon from the 1860s onward.[^13] Between the Ras el-Tin daguerreotype (1839), the Saint-Louis one (1842-1843), and Washington's studio in Monrovia (1853-late 1850s), some fifteen years passed — and photography, in West Africa as elsewhere on the continent, remained, almost everywhere, a practice imported from elsewhere.

:::encart What this episode could not show

Itier's own Saint-Louis plate has no freely accessible digital reproduction to date — the French holdings that preserve it are, for the most part, not yet digitised online. One lead was examined and set aside: a set of engravings from the Raffenel expedition (1843-1844), held at Gallica/BnF, whose own catalogue describes it as a series of ethnographic portraits of Senegal's inhabitants rather than a view of the town, and whose reuse terms are explicitly restricted rather than open. Rather than illustrate this section with an image that matches neither the subject nor this blog's sourcing standards, we chose to leave it without an image — as episode 1 did for the 1839 plate, also lost.

:::

Ultimately, these two 1840s-1850s trajectories trace less a continuity than a contrast: on one side, a colonial administration documenting in passing; on the other, a man who chose West Africa as home and built there, with the same technique, a body of work and a life. It would take another decade, with figures such as Francis W. Joaque, for a photography held by Africans themselves to emerge — the subject of this series' next episode.

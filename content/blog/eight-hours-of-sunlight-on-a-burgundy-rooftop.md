We should start by saying what it looks like, because it is disappointing.

A pewter plate, twenty centimetres by sixteen, coated in a brownish substance. On it, shapes that take a moment to resolve: the slope of a roof on the left, a tower or a dovecote in the middle, a tree, the wing of a barn on the right. Between them, an empty courtyard. The image is blurred, greasy, close to illegible. If someone showed it to you without a word, you would think of a botched engraving, or a stain.

It is the oldest surviving photograph in the world. It was made around 1826 or 1827 by a Burgundian inventor named Nicéphore Niépce, from the window of an upper room in his house at Le Gras, near Saint-Loup-de-Varennes. It is called Point de vue du Gras. Today it sits at the University of Texas at Austin, in a case filled with inert gas, lit as little as possible.

And it contains an anomaly that almost nobody notices.

## The sun that lights both sides

Look at the shadows.

On the left-hand wall, the light comes from the right. On the building to the right, it comes from the left. Both faces of the courtyard are lit at once, which is physically impossible: one sun, one direction, at any given instant.

Except there is no given instant. That is the whole of it.

Niépce worked with bitumen of Judea, an asphalt derivative spread in a thin layer across the plate. The bitumen hardens where light strikes it; the rest, still soluble, is washed away afterwards. It is a process of unimaginable slowness. Niépce spoke of an eight-hour exposure; researchers who have tried to reproduce his method believe it may have taken him several days. Either way the result is the same: the plate did not record a moment, it recorded a duration. The sun crossed the sky while the image was forming, lighting first one façade and then the other, and the bitumen kept both.

What we call the first photograph is therefore not an image of what could be seen through that window. It is an image of an entire day, stacked on itself. An object no human eye could ever have seen.

## The founding misunderstanding

We have grown used to thinking of photography as extraction. You press, you cut out a piece of the real, you carry it away. Everyday language says so plainly: we take a picture, we capture a moment, we immortalise a scene. All those words assume that something already existed, whole, and that the camera merely seized it.

The very first image of the medium contradicts that idea before it has had time to settle. It extracts nothing. It manufactures.

And this is not some flaw of youth that technique has since cured. Every photograph you make this week rests on the same operation. A long exposure over a river turns water into mist — nobody has ever seen a river that way. An exposure at 1/4000th freezes a droplet into a glass bead — nobody has ever seen that either. A telephoto lens compresses the planes and pulls a mountain up against a village ten kilometres away. A wide angle does the opposite. Black and white removes information that every one of your senses supplies constantly.

Each time, the camera produces a reality with no equivalent in experience. Niépce was simply the first, and in the most visible way imaginable.

## What it changes for the person photographing

We could stop there, content with the historical curiosity. But this anomaly has a very practical consequence, and it holds as much for an amateur with a phone as for a professional with a medium format.

If photography does not extract but manufactures, then the question "is it faithful?" is badly put. No image is. The right question is: **what am I choosing to manufacture, and why?**

It is a modest shift in appearance, a considerable one in practice. It moves you from the stance of witness — I am here, I record — to the stance of author: I decide what this scene is going to become. The frame excludes more than it includes, and that exclusion is a sentence. Exposure time is not a technical setting but a statement about the nature of the world: unstable or still, rushed or suspended. The moment you press does not catch an instant that existed on its own; it creates one, by declaring that this is the one that counts.

Many people who describe themselves as stuck in their photographic practice are stuck exactly there. They have learned the settings, they own a good camera, their images are sharp and correctly exposed — and they say nothing, because they still believe themselves to be witnesses. They are waiting for the world to hand them something to record. It never does. The world supplies only raw material.

## The image that nearly disappeared

There is one last thing to know about this plate, and it is not a footnote. Niépce died in 1833 without ever profiting from his invention. His associate, Louis Daguerre, developed a faster and more legible process a few years later, which took his name and absorbed all the light. Niépce's name slid slowly out of official history. As for the Gras plate, it was lost from view for decades, forgotten in a family trunk, exhibited once in London in 1898 and then nothing. It was not found again until 1952, by a photography historian named Helmut Gernsheim, after years of searching and a stubborn correspondence.

One hundred and twenty-five years between the moment an image is made and the moment we understand what it is.

It is a pattern that will return often in this series. Vivian Maier never showed a single one of her photographs in her lifetime. William Eggleston was demolished by the critics before being recognised as a master. Saul Leiter spent forty years preferring silence. Photography has a strange relationship with time — not only inside the image, where eight hours of sunlight fit on a pewter plate, but around it too, in the delay it takes for a way of seeing to be understood.

Which should, incidentally, bring some peace to anyone photographing today without an audience.

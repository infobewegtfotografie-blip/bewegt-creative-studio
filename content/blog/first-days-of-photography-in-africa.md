On 19 August 1839, in a crowded hall at the Institut de France, the astronomer and physicist François Arago officially unveiled the daguerreotype process on behalf of Louis-Jacques-Mandé Daguerre, before the assembled Académie des sciences and Académie des beaux-arts. The news spread like wildfire: "a few days later, black three-legged boxes could be seen planted in every square in Paris, in front of churches and palaces," reported a German witness present that day.[^1] The French government, which had bought Daguerre's patent on 1 August 1839 to give it "nobly to the whole world," hoped from the outset for a scientific use of the process: to document, at last "objectively," the remains of Antiquity.

Arago himself had singled out Egypt as the natural proving ground for the new technique, publicly regretting that daguerreotypy had not existed at the time of Bonaparte's expedition: "Everyone will grasp the extraordinary advantages that could have been drawn, during the Egyptian expedition, from a means of reproduction so exact and so rapid," he told the Académie.[^1] The publication, between 1809 and 1829, of the twenty-four volumes of the *Description de l'Égypte* — drawn from the work of the scholars who had accompanied Bonaparte — had already fed a lasting French fascination with the Nile valley. It is therefore no surprise that African soil received the light of the daguerreotype before almost any other continent outside Europe.

## Lerebours's commission and the departure for Alexandria

The Parisian optician Noël-Marie Paymal Lerebours, eager to assemble the first great photographic collection of the world's monuments, equipped several amateur travelers with cameras and chemicals. He entrusted a mission to the orientalist painter Horace Vernet, then director of the Académie de France in Rome, and to his nephew Frédéric Goupil-Fesquet (1817-1878), tasked with producing views to complement his uncle's sketches.[^1][^2] The two men left Marseille on 21 October 1839 — barely two months after Arago's presentation — stopping in Italy, Malta, and Greece before landing in Alexandria on 4 November 1839.

On 6 November, the French consul introduced them to Méhémet Ali, viceroy of Egypt, reclining "on his divan, in a corner of the salon," as Goupil-Fesquet would recount in his *Voyage d'Horace Vernet en Orient*, published in Paris in 1843. The ruler, curious about the progress of the arts and sciences in Europe, expressed a wish to see the new instrument at work. A meeting was arranged for the following morning, at the Ras el-Tin palace on the harbor of Alexandria.

## 7 November 1839: the first documented photograph of the African continent

It was on 7 November 1839, at seven in the morning, in front of the gates of the Ras el-Tin palace, that Vernet and Goupil-Fesquet made the view that several historians of photography now consider the first documented photograph of Egypt — and, more broadly, of the African continent.[^1] The original plate is now lost; the image survives only as an engraving made after it, showing a fortified gate with two men standing before it, a guard and a doorkeeper, with the palace harem and its low roofs in the background.

The scene of the shoot itself was preserved in memory thanks to Goupil-Fesquet's account. The operation — sensitizing the plate with iodine vapor, exposure, development with mercury vapor, then fixing with sodium hyposulfite — took several minutes under Méhémet Ali's wary gaze.

:::encart "It is the work of the devil!"

So Méhémet Ali is said to have reacted to the daguerreotype, according to the account left by Frédéric Goupil-Fesquet in his *Voyage d'Horace Vernet en Orient* (1843). The viceroy, at first frightened, was soon won over — enough, in fact, to learn to operate the camera himself.

## An Egyptian fever

The two Frenchmen did not stop there. On 20 November 1839, Goupil-Fesquet captured the pyramid of Khufu at Giza after a fifteen-minute exposure — "we are daguerreotyping like lions," Vernet wrote to a correspondent at the time.[^1] In Alexandria, they crossed paths with a third daguerreotypist, the Swiss-Canadian Pierre-Gustave Joly de Lotbinière, also commissioned by Lerebours, who had just made, in Athens in mid-October 1839, the very first photographic views of the Acropolis before moving on to Egypt. He would continue alone southward, photographing Karnak, the Colossi of Memnon, Philae, and Abu Simbel, for a claimed total of ninety-two views.

None of this pioneering trio's original plates survived: we know them only through the engravings published between 1840 and 1844 in Lerebours's *Excursions daguerriennes: vues et monuments les plus remarquables du globe*, and in Hector Horeau's *Panorama d'Égypte et de Nubie* (1841) for Joly de Lotbinière's views.

## Girault de Prangey: the largest photographic undertaking of its time

Three years later, the architect and daguerreotypist Joseph-Philibert Girault de Prangey (1804-1892) set out, from 1842, on a three-year journey across the eastern Mediterranean — Greece, Egypt, Turkey, Syria, Lebanon — carrying his daguerreotype equipment. Arriving in Egypt in the autumn of 1842, he explored Alexandria and Rosetta before settling in Cairo, where he spent several months photographing Islamic and pharaonic architecture. In total, Girault de Prangey brought back more than a thousand daguerreotypes, a body of work without equal for its time.[^3]

Unlike the plates of Vernet, Goupil-Fesquet, and Joly de Lotbinière, a significant portion of Girault de Prangey's daguerreotypes survived: they are today the oldest surviving photographs — not merely known through engravings — of Egypt and the Near East. Left largely forgotten on the family estate in Langres after the photographer's death, they did not resurface until the twentieth century and were only fully revealed to the public quite recently, through conservation work and museum exhibitions.[^3] Girault de Prangey also made, during this journey, some of the very first photographic portraits taken in Africa.

## Southward: the daguerreotype reaches the Cape Colony

It would take another seven years for daguerreotypy to cross the African equator. According to historian Patricia Hayes (University of the Western Cape), the process "travelled quickly across this liquid frontier" formed by colonial shipping routes, reaching Durban via Mauritius in September 1846.[^4][^5] Unlike Talbot's English calotype, the French patent was protected by no monopoly, so it spread unhindered after 1839 across the French-speaking colonies and their commercial spheres of influence.

It was the Frenchman Jules Léger who landed, aboard the schooner *Hannah Codner*, at Algoa Bay (present-day Port Elizabeth), where by October 1846 he was exhibiting a series of portraits of settlers and local views, described by the *Grahamstown Journal* of November 1846 as "magnificent, wonderful, interesting."[^4] His associate William Ring then carried the equipment on to Cape Town, with less success. It was not until 1851 that three daguerreotypists — Carel Sparmann, William Waller, and John Paul — set up lastingly profitable studios there.[^4][^5] Hayes notes that "there is no evidence of photographs taken before 1845" at the Cape, the technology having taken several years to cross the sea distance separating Europe from the southern tip of the continent.

## The case of French North Africa: a patchier record

The story of the daguerreotype's arrival in Algeria, a French colony since 1830, is far less well established than that of Egypt or the Cape. Historians of colonial photography, foremost among them David Prochaska (University of Illinois), have shown that photography did not become a systematic instrument of documentation there — administrative, military, ethnographic, touristic — until the second half of the nineteenth century, the output of the 1840s remaining fragmentary and scattered across private collections and still poorly catalogued archives.[^6] This documentary asymmetry alone illustrates how hard it is to write a continuous history of photography in Africa in the nineteenth century.

## "The first photograph of Africa": the wrong question?

Should we, then, stop at this date of 7 November 1839, and at this palace in Alexandria photographed by two passing Frenchmen? Historians specializing in African photography urge caution. In their foundational article, David Killingray and Andrew Roberts note that most of the continent's photographic output in the early decades remained the work of European travelers, missionaries, and administrators, and that the sources available for this period "remain extremely uneven from one region to another."[^7]

Erin Haney, in *Photography and Africa* (2010), goes further: she explicitly rejects the project of a linear, exhaustive history of African photography, insisting on the need to situate each image within its socio-political conditions of production[^8] — a warning that applies with even more force to the very notion of a "first photo," which presupposes an outside gaze, that of the Western traveler who came to document monuments deemed worthy of interest by criteria of his own. Jürg Schneider shows, conversely, that genuinely African photography — that is, made by African photographers, for local clienteles — only emerges in the second half of the nineteenth century, with figures such as Francis W. Joaque, active in Sierra Leone, Fernando Po, and then Gabon from the 1860s onward.[^9]

There are, in reality, two distinct histories that too often get collapsed under the convenient label "the early days of photography in Africa": one, well documented, of the daguerreotype's arrival by the colonial and touristic route — of which the Ras el-Tin palace view of 7 November 1839 constitutes, on present evidence, the earliest and best-attested milestone — and another, later and harder to reconstruct, of the medium's appropriation by African societies themselves.

## Conclusion

As matters currently stand, the photograph of the Ras el-Tin palace made on 7 November 1839 by Horace Vernet and Frédéric Goupil-Fesquet remains the first documented image of the African continent — a vanished document, known only through an engraving, taken barely eleven weeks after the process was publicly unveiled in Paris. It opened a decade of orientalist daguerreotypy in Egypt, carried forward by Joly de Lotbinière and then, on a far larger scale, by Girault de Prangey, before the process reached, at the slower pace of colonial shipping routes, southern Africa from 1846 onward. But this chronology, precise as it is, should not obscure a more complex reality: that of a medium taking several decades to become truly African, in the sense of Africans taking it up themselves as practitioners, and not merely as subjects.
